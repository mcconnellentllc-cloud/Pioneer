Real Questions, Instant Answers

Here's what it actually looks like. These are questions you'd normally call someone about — and the kind of answer AI gives you on the spot.

"What's the recommended seeding rate for Pioneer P1185AM corn at 7,800 feet elevation on dryland in NE Colorado?"
For dryland corn at 7,800 ft in NE Colorado, target 16,000–20,000 seeds/acre for P1185AM. The lower end of that range is safer in drier years with limited stored moisture. If you had good fall/winter precipitation and have 3+ feet of stored soil moisture, push toward 20,000. At your elevation the growing season is shorter, so the 85-day maturity of P1185AM is a good fit. Drop population if planting after May 15.
The old way: Call your seed rep, wait for a callback, hope they remember your specific field conditions.
"I need the part number for the gauge wheel arm on a John Deere 1770NT 16-row planter."
The gauge wheel arm assembly for a JD 1770NT is part number AA74462 (right) and AA74461 (left). The pivot bolt is H150888. If you're replacing the arm because of wear at the pivot point, also check the bushing (N283805) — that's usually what wears first and it's a $12 part vs. replacing the whole arm at ~$180.
The old way: Call the dealer parts counter, get put on hold, spell your name twice, wait while they look it up in the catalog.
"What's the custom rate for combining corn in eastern Colorado? I need to know what to charge."
Based on the 2025 Iowa State and CSU Extension custom rate surveys, combining corn in the Central Great Plains runs $38–$48/acre for the combine only, or $52–$68/acre for combine + trucking + auger cart. For NE Colorado specifically, you're on the higher end because of field distances and lower average yields (meaning more acres per bushel of revenue for the customer). A competitive rate for 2026 would be $42–$46/acre combine-only or $58–$65/acre full-service. Add $0.04–$0.06/bushel if you're doing the grain cart too.
The old way: Call three other operators, ask what they charge, hope they tell you the truth, then guess.
"When is the final planting date for crop insurance on dryland corn in Phillips County, Colorado?"
For Phillips County, CO (county code 121), the final planting date for dryland corn under USDA crop insurance is May 31. The late planting period runs June 1–June 25, with a 1% per day reduction in your guarantee for each day planted after May 31. After June 25, you'd need to file for prevented planting if you still can't get in the field. For irrigated corn it's also May 31. Check your specific policy — some plans may differ.
The old way: Call your crop insurance agent, leave a voicemail, wait a day, call again.
"What's the tank mix for spraying volunteer corn out of soybeans? I've got Roundup Ready beans."
For volunteer RR corn in Roundup Ready soybeans, Roundup (glyphosate) won't kill the corn since it's also RR. Use a grass herbicide: Select Max (clethodim) at 12–16 oz/acre + COC at 1% v/v, or Assure II (quizalofop) at 10–12 oz/acre + COC or NIS. Select Max is cheaper (~$6–8/acre) and more forgiving on timing. Spray when the volunteer corn is 6–12 inches tall. Don't wait until it's tasseling — at that point you'll need the high rate and it still might not die clean. Add AMS at 2.5 lb/acre if you have hard water.
The old way: Call your chem supplier, wait for the agronomist to call back, then double-check the label yourself anyway.
"How many gallons per minute does a Zimmatic 7-tower center pivot need to water 126 acres of corn at peak demand?"
At peak demand (late July, tasseling/silking), corn needs about 0.35 inches/day in NE Colorado. For a 126-acre Zimmatic 7-tower system, that means you need to apply ~0.35"/day with some extra for efficiency losses. At 85% application efficiency: 126 acres × 0.35" ÷ 0.85 = 51.9 acre-inches/day. That's about 800 GPM running 24 hours, or ~900 GPM running 21 hours (with time for position changes). If your well does 700 GPM, you can still keep up by running the pivot slower and accepting a slightly longer rotation — just don't let rotation time exceed 3.5 days at peak or you'll stress the crop.
The old way: Call your irrigation dealer, wait for their tech to run the numbers, get a callback in two days when you need the answer now.

Tips for Talking to AI

Be Specific

Instead of "help with marketing," say "write a Facebook post about our Pioneer seed plot day on June 15 in Haxtun." The more details, the better the answer.

Give Context

Tell the AI who you are: "I'm a farmer in NE Colorado with 2,000 dryland acres." This helps it give you relevant, local answers.

Ask Follow-Ups

Don't settle for the first answer. Say "make it shorter," "add pricing," or "now do one for soybeans." The conversation keeps going.

Double-Check Facts

AI is a great starting point, but always verify important numbers, dates, and claims — especially for chemical rates, regulations, or legal matters.
