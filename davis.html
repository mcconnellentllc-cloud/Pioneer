Setup Guide

Step 1 — Pick the right station

For agriculture, you need the solar radiation sensor — it drives ET (evapotranspiration) calculations. That means you want the Vantage Pro2 Plus, not the standard Vantage Pro2.

Recommended Vantage Pro2 Plus + WeatherLink Console (SKU 6262) — includes UV & solar radiation sensors, wireless, plus the Console for WiFi and display
Already own a station? You only need the WeatherLink Console (SKU 6313) to get your existing Vantage Pro2 or Vue online.

Step 2 — Mount the station

Sensor placement directly affects your data quality. For accurate ET and rainfall, follow these guidelines.

Overall Height

Mount the sensor suite at 5 to 6 feet above ground. Easy to maintain, accurate temperature/humidity readings.

Wind Sensor

Mount the anemometer at 6 feet (2 meters) — this is the reference height for the Penman-Monteith ET equation. Can be up to 40 ft away using the included cable.

Rain Gauge

Needs open sky above — no branches or overhangs. Keep it at least 4x the height of any nearby obstruction away (20 ft building = 80 ft minimum distance).

Solar Radiation Sensor

Needs clear view of the sky from horizon to horizon. Keep the lens clean — dust and bird droppings degrade ET accuracy.

For ag: Install over well-irrigated, mowed grass if possible. Avoid pavement, buildings, or anything that creates a heat island. The station should be representative of your actual field conditions.

Step 3 — Set up the WeatherLink Console

The WeatherLink Console is the brains — it receives wireless data from the outdoor sensors, displays it on a color touchscreen, and uploads everything to the cloud over WiFi.

  1. Plug the Console into a USB power source (standard phone charger works). It has a backup battery for outages.
  2. On first power-up, the Console walks you through an on-screen setup wizard.
  3. Connect to your WiFi network (2.4 GHz only — most routers support this). The Console can show local sensor data without WiFi, but WiFi is required for cloud uploads and app access.
  4. Tap "Add stations and sensors" on the Console screen.
  5. Select the transmitter ID that matches your outdoor sensor suite (check the label inside the transmitter shelter if needed).
  6. Choose "Vantage Pro2 sensor suite" (or Vantage Vue if that's what you have).
  7. Select your Vantage Pro2 package type from the list and check the sensors you have installed.
  8. Select your rain collector type — choose 0.01" (standard US).
  9. Give your station a name (e.g., "McConnell Home Quarter").
  10. Data should appear on the Console screen within a couple of minutes. You're receiving locally.

Step 4 — Create your WeatherLink account & register

  1. Go to weatherlink.com and click "Sign Up Free".
  2. Enter your name, email, and a password. Follow the email verification prompt.
  3. Once logged in, click the green "Add Devices" button at the top of the page.
  4. Follow the prompts to link your Console — enter the Device ID shown on the Console screen. On the Console: tap the gear icon, then "About" to find the Device ID.
  5. Your station data should start appearing on weatherlink.com within minutes.
Free vs. Pro: The free Basic plan uploads every 15 minutes and gives you current conditions, charts, and third-party uploads. The Pro plan ($3.95/month) uploads every 1–5 minutes and adds data download and advanced charting.

Step 5 — Download the WeatherLink app

View your station data on your phone from anywhere. When you're on the same WiFi as the Console, the app updates every 2.5 seconds — you can watch wind gusts in real time.

Step 6 — Connect to Weather Underground

Get your station on the Weather Underground map so neighbors, other growers, and the public can see your local conditions. This also feeds your data into the Weather Underground app and forecasts.

  1. Go to wunderground.com/signup and create a free account (or log in if you have one).
  2. Go to My Weather Stations and click "Add a New PWS".
  3. Use the map to pinpoint your station's exact location — drag the marker to where the station is physically installed.
  4. Enter your station's actual elevation in the field provided.
  5. Fill in the station details and click "Submit".
  6. You'll receive a Station ID (looks like KCOHAXT##) and a Station Key. Write both down. You'll need these in the next step. The Station Key is case-sensitive.
  7. Now go to weatherlink.com and log in.
  8. Click "Share & Uploads" in the top menu, then click "Upload".
  9. Click "Add Upload" and select "Weather Underground" from the dropdown.
  10. Enter your Station ID and Station Key. Type them manually — copy/paste can add invisible spaces that break it. Double-check: don't confuse the letter O with zero, or lowercase L with the number 1.
  11. Save. Your data should appear on Weather Underground within the hour at wunderground.com/dashboard/pws/YOUR_STATION_ID.

Step 7 — Share to the Corteva Weather Pillar

  1. Log in at https://www.weatherlink.com/
  2. Click the Station icon
  3. Click Share & Uploads
  4. In the pop-up, select Add Share
  5. Search Corteva Weather and click Search
  6. Leave default Basic Share, then click Share
Troubleshooting

How to tell if your station is losing signal

The outdoor sensor suite (ISS) transmits wirelessly to the Console every 2.5 seconds. When that signal weakens or drops, here's what you'll see.

Dashes (--) on display Where you'd normally see temperature, humidity, wind, etc., you see dashes instead of numbers. This is the most obvious sign — the Console hasn't received data for 10+ minutes.
"X" flashing Normal. An X flashes each time the Console successfully receives a data packet. If you see this, your signal is fine.
"R" displayed Reacquire mode. The Console is trying to re-establish connection after 20 consecutive missed packets. It will try for up to 10 minutes.
"L" displayed Lost mode. The Console gave up trying and is sleeping for 15 minutes before it retries. If you see "L", you have a real signal problem.
"Low battery transmitter 1" The ISS backup battery (CR-123A) needs replacement. A weak battery means the transmitter can't send a strong signal, especially at night when the solar panel isn't charging.
WeatherLink Health tab On weatherlink.com or the app, check the Health tab. RSSI (signal strength) is color-coded: Green = good (better than −90 dBm), Yellow = weak (−90 to −99 dBm), Red = poor (worse than −99 dBm). Also shows packet reception percentage since midnight.

Check signal strength on the Console

  1. Press and hold TEMP, then press HUM to enter Diagnostic Mode.
  2. Press 2ND, then press CHILL (Wind Chill) to open the Reception Diagnostic Screen.
  3. Look at signal strength (top right) — values between 20 and 60. Higher is better. If it shows dashes, the most recent packet was missed.
  4. Check background noise — also 5 to 60. Signal strength needs to be higher than noise for clear reception.
  5. Check reception percentage — should be 95%+. The bar graph shows daily reception over the last 24 days.
  6. Check resync count — a high number means chronic signal problems.
WeatherLink Console (6313): Tap the gear icon, then Device Information. It shows RSSI in dBm for each transmitter. Tap any transmitter line for detailed reception data.

Common causes of weak signal

Distance

Rated for 1,000 ft line of sight, but real-world range through walls is only 200–400 ft. Metal buildings cut it even further.

Metal Obstructions

Metal roofing, siding, window frames, barns, and silos block signal. Each wall the signal passes through weakens it. Metal buildings are the worst.

RF Interference

900 MHz cordless phones are the most common culprit. Also older baby monitors, other weather stations, and some wireless routers near the Console.

Low ISS Battery

The CR-123A backup battery powers the transmitter when solar isn't enough (night, cloudy days, winter). A weak battery = weak signal, especially at night. Replace every 2 years.

Fixes & solutions

  1. Move the Console closer to a window facing the ISS — glass is mostly transparent to the radio signal. A windowsill on the side facing the station is the easiest fix.
  2. Get the Console higher — second floor or higher shelf. Signal travels better above ground clutter.
  3. Reduce wall penetrations — arrange the signal path so it goes through a window or fewer walls. Avoid routing through metal buildings entirely.
  4. Check antenna alignment — both antennas (Console and ISS) should be vertical and parallel. If the ISS is on a hill above the Console, try both horizontal.
  5. Kill the interference — move 900 MHz cordless phones, wireless routers, and other electronics away from the Console. Check background noise in diagnostic mode.
  6. Replace the ISS battery — open the SIM box (white box with the solar panel), swap the CR-123A lithium battery. Inspect for corrosion on the contacts while it's open. Davis part number 8415. Battery warning may not clear until midnight.
  7. Add a Davis Wireless Repeater (SKU 7627) — receives the ISS signal, boosts it, and retransmits it. Solar powered. Adds up to 1,000 ft per hop. You can chain up to 4 repeaters. ~$495–$575. Contact Kyle for partner pricing.
Night-only signal loss? That usually means the ISS battery is dying. The solar panel keeps the transmitter running during the day, but at night the backup battery takes over — and if it's weak, the transmitter drops out. Replace the CR-123A and the problem should clear up.
